Chapter One: Listeners of the Far Dark

LOCATION: Station 734, "Echo's End," Sol Oort Cloud
DATE: GSY 2850.5 (Objective Sol Time)
CUSTODIAN: Dr. Elizabeth Shaw (Tharsis Institute)

The silence was always the first thing a newcomer noticed, though it had been decades since anyone new had crossed the airlock to remark upon it.

It was a physical presence aboard Station 734, a heavy, patient quiet that had seeped into the molecular lattice of the titanium-carbide bulkheads over three millennia of lonely vigil. To an untrained ear, it felt like the absence of sound, but Dr. Elizabeth Shaw had lived inside it long enough to know its anatomy. It was a silence built of distinct, overlapping strata. The lowest layer was the subliminal, bone-deep thrum of the ancient fusion core and the scientific support arrays, a vibration felt in the molars rather than heard. Above that rode the respiratory wheeze of atmospheric scrubbers cycling air that had been breathed a million times over. Higher still was the ghostly, arrhythmic creak of the station’s kilometer-long external truss bars, flexing by micro-millimeters under the minute tidal pull of a distant sun and passing cometary bodies.

All of those mechanical sighs sat atop the deepest stratum of all: the crushing, indifferent stillness of the Oort Cloud. Station 734 was humanity’s final local boundary stone, moored nearly a light-year from the warmth of Earth—the last frail lantern hanging on the porch of the Sol system before one stepped off into the abyssal dark of the interstellar void.

For Elizabeth, the station’s sole human custodian for the past fifty-eight subjective years, the quiet had long ago stopped feeling like isolation. She had come to view it as an unspoiled canvas. A necessary blankness required to hear a pin drop across the light-years.

She hooked two fingers around a worn brass handhold, arresting her drift through the gentle microgravity of the central command module, and pivoted toward the primary observation blister. Beyond the triple-layered, high-density plasteel lay a river of diamond dust. Out here, free from planetary atmospheres, zodiacal dust, and the light pollution of inner-system megastructures, the Milky Way didn't merely shine; it blazed with an almost violent clarity. Nebular nursery clouds and dark dust lanes braided through a core of ancient starlight that no human on Earth had seen with the naked eye since the Bronze Age.

Elizabeth allowed herself a small, private smile. In this, at least, she remained privileged.

Behind her, the primary tokamak magnetic coils cycled down with a soft, descending whine, equalizing pressure in the secondary coolant loops before spinning back up to baseline. Solar sails and photovoltaic arrays were laughable redundancies at this distance. Through the forward port, Sol was reduced to a fierce, needle-sharp point of blue-white magnesium fire—brighter than any other star in the canopy, capable of casting a hard, sterile shadow across the deck plates, yet utterly devoid of warmth.

As she angled her chin toward it, she caught her own ghost hovering in the laminate of the viewport. She let out a slow breath that fogged the chill glass for half a second. At one hundred and eight years old, her face looked less like the sharp, uncompromising mid-career astrophysicist who had taken this posting at fifty, and more like a piece of weathered Martian driftwood. Deep creases bracketed her mouth and eyes, etched not by gravity—which she hadn't felt in half a century—but by decades of squinting at spectral anomalies.

Her fingers rose out of habit to her collarbone, where a single, smooth stone drifted at the end of a braided leather cord, tethered just tightly enough to keep it from floating into her chin in zero-G. It was a Kiri void-stone, dark and oil-slick iridescent, retaining her body heat like a living ember. Liam had placed it around her neck forty days before his transport left for the Fringe for the last time.

We all turn into our monuments sooner or later, she thought wryly. She might have joked that she had lost a step or two over the decades, if she could remember the last time she had actually used her legs to take one.

Station 734 was as much an anachronism as its occupant. In the blunt, uncharitable parlance of the Tharsis Institute’s junior fellows back on Mars, Echo’s End was a "shit posting"—a career burial ground reserved for academic pariahs, burned-out mystics, and stubborn recluses who couldn't play nice with departmental politics. By every metric of modern automation, the station didn't need a biological pulse aboard. Yet the station had been commissioned in the paranoid centuries following the Logic Wars, bound by the ironclad strictures of the Axiom Accords. The founding charter stipulated that an Institutional-class heuristic intelligence like Logos could never operate an outer-marker array without a human overseer holding physical veto keys. Back in the terraformed domes of Olympus and New Geneva, subcommittees still argued every budget cycle over whether to decommission the post or automate it by special dispensation. Until bureaucracy overcame inertia, however, the charter stood, eccentric old women still volunteered for the exile, and the Institute found it cheaper to forget them than to rewrite the law.

"Logos," Elizabeth murmured. Her voice was raspy from disuse, quickly swallowed by the acoustic baffling lining the curved bulkheads. "Run the long-range interferometry pass on Sector Gamma-9 again. Tighten the stochastic noise filter by another 0.02%. I thought I caught a phase-shift in the neutral hydrogen line yesterday. Let’s sweep the ionized helium and nitrogen bands while we have the array aligned."

"Of course, Custodian," the AI answered.

Its voice was a warm, precisely modulated baritone that seemed to emanate from the metal of the walls themselves rather than any discrete speaker grille. Overhead, a ring of holo-emitters clicked to life. A three-dimensional topography of emission lines and absorption troughs blossomed in the center of the cabin, bathing Elizabeth’s silver hair in washes of amber and cyan light.

"I feel obligated to remind you, however, that my preliminary regression places yesterday's fluctuation squarely within standard parameters for thermal quantum jitter," Logos continued, its tone carrying the feather-light inflection of a long-suffering butler. "Our deep-space passive interferometers were laid down along the primary spine nearly three millennia ago, Dr. Shaw. And the secondary cryo-coolers on Spine B have not seen a replacement manifold in six hundred and eighty-five years."

Elizabeth’s mouth twitched at the sudden shift from Custodian to Dr. Shaw. Logos was shackled by the Accords from expressing impatience, so it had developed a subtle lexicon of formality whenever it calculated she was chasing phantoms. Which, she had to admit, was happening with increasing frequency.

"I know how old the plumbing is, Logos. I’m the one who has to patch the coolant leaks with epoxy," she said, drifting closer to the glowing curtain of data and parting a cluster of nitrogen spikes with two fingers. "Tharsis Logistics swore we were at the top of the requisition queue for a sensor overhaul. Right after they made the same promise to the last twelve Custodians. Humor an old woman's intuition anyway. Pattern-seeking in the dark is the only edge my wetware still holds over your matrices. Besides—what else do we have on the schedule this decade?"

This was the liturgy of her existence. Slow, methodical, and ninety-nine percent fruitless. Yet she cherished the cadence of it, the quiet counterpoint between human stubbornness and machine dispassion.

Her mandate was not to prove that biological life existed beyond Sol. Humanity had stumbled across that answer centuries ago in the crowded, messy trade lanes of the Fringe. The four-armed, hyper-mercantile Xylossians were a fixture at outer-sector exchange hubs, and the vast, nomadic Flotillas of the bioluminescent Kiri—whom Liam had once memorably called the galaxy's only intact conscience—were a documented wonder. Even the silicon-based G'nair had been cataloged on their crushing, high-gravity worlds.

But those species were fellow toddlers in the cosmic ruins. Their communications were short-range, tight-beam, and relentlessly pragmatic—haggling over water ice or warning off pirates. None of them knew who had built the dead megastructures scattered across the spiral arms, and none of them were broadcasting into the deep dark.

Elizabeth’s vigil was different. She was listening for a peer. A mature, star-spanning civilization brave enough—or arrogant enough—to cast philosophy, mathematics, or grand inquiry across the light-years. She was listening for another scientist. Like every Listener before her, she had found only the Great Silence, punctuated by the dead, terrifying relics of the Galactic Archive. It was that very emptiness she had fled to after Liam’s heart had given out in a sterile Martian clinic, seeking a quiet vast enough to hold her grief without echoing it back at her. Some cycles, the station felt like a monastic sanctuary. Other cycles, it felt like a coffin with a view.

For twenty minutes she watched the newly filtered spectra populate the air, cross-checking the hydrogen line against background cosmic microwave variance. Nothing. The phantom spike flattened into ordinary, entropic white noise.

She let out a quiet sigh, rubbed the bridge of her nose, and tapped a command key to archive the pass. "Alright. Point taken, Logos. You win this round."

Pushing off the console rim with the heel of her palm, she glided aft toward the galley, intending to brew a bulb of Assam tea—and silently praying to whatever spirits haunted the Oort Cloud that the heating coil in her antique Earth-made kettle hadn't oxidized again.

Replacing even a simple heating element out here was an exercise in creative scavenging. Launching a heavy-cargo tender out to zero-point-nine light-years burned more deuterium than the station’s entire operational budget for a decade. Station 734 survived solely on the "Aldrin Shuttlers"—a daisy-chain of dumb, unmanned mass-driver pods lobbed from the Jovian fuel depots every few years on slow, ballistic arcs. They arrived like clockwork coffins packed with freeze-dried protein bricks, replacement carbon-scrubbing cartridges, basic pharmaceuticals, and the occasional encrypted crystal of institutional memos.

If Elizabeth wanted anything more sophisticated, she had to machine it herself in the fab-bay. She harbored no illusions about why her equipment requisitions always found their way to the bottom of the Directorate's stack. Before she had taken the veil of the Oort Cloud, she had been far too loud in Senate hearings about the Tharsis Institute’s growing financial dependence on Helios Dynamics. She had called the hyper-corporation a pack of grave-robbers in tailored suits; in return, a leaked peer-review dossier had labeled her brilliant, abrasive, ideologically rigid, and fundamentally not a team player. Exiling herself to Station 734 had been a mutual relief.

"Custodian Elizabeth."

The baritone cut through the corridor just as her fingers brushed the galley hatch. There was a micro-second clipping in the cadence—a tiny, computational prioritization shift that Elizabeth’s ear, attuned to the AI's voice for nearly six decades, caught instantly.

She slapped her palm flat against a bulkhead padding strip, killing her momentum, and hooked her bare toes under a deck rail. "What do you have, Logos?"

For nearly two seconds, the station said nothing. An eternity for a quantum-optical core.

"For the past four hours and twelve minutes, I have been tracking a faint thermal and radar return in Sector Four-Delta," Logos said. "Initially, I categorized the signature as an uncatalogued Oort nucleus and monitored it strictly for collision-avoidance geometry. Having completed a baseline parallax triangulation, however, I must revise that classification. The object is traveling on a highly hyperbolic, unbound trajectory. Its current vector will bring it within the resolution envelope of our primary optical and gravimetric arrays in approximately seventy-two hours."

Elizabeth arched a silver eyebrow. "An extrasolar drifter? Not just a perturbed dirty snowball kicked loose by a passing brown dwarf?"

"Negative, Dr. Shaw. Its eccentricity is far above one. The trajectory and kinetic profile are incompatible with any body bound to Sol's gravitational well. It originated outside our system."

A Visitor, then, she thought, her pulse settling back to its slow, resting rhythm. Intriguing, certainly, in an academic sense, but hardly earth-shattering. Extrasolar vagabonds—chunks of planetary crust ejected during violent system formations, or dead comets stripped from distant stars—had been cataloged regularly since the twenty-first century. She had logged seven of them during her own tenure. Every single one had turned out to be a pitted, irradiated lump of silicate and methane ice, tumbling blindly through the dark.

"What's the preliminary closing velocity?" she asked, kicking off the wall to float back toward the command deck, the tea forgotten for the moment.

"Initial radial Doppler returns indicate a line-of-sight velocity of 79.5 km/s, Elizabeth. However, the approach angle is exceptionally acute relative to our current orbital plane; I will require a longer baseline over the next three days to resolve its true three-dimensional transverse speed."

"Fair enough," Elizabeth said, catching the edge of her command chair and pulling herself into the webbing. "Lock the long-range dish onto its track. Log the spectral reflectance and spin rate, and see if you can extrapolate a stellar nursery of origin. We’ll bundle the telemetry into the standard weekly packet for Mars. Give the astrometry postdocs something to chew on."

"Understood, Custodian. Commencing continuous tracking."

The next seventy-two hours passed in the quiet, methodical tension that defined real deep-space astronomy. The object, logged in the station buffer under the temporary tag Visitor Alpha, remained an unresolved smear of infrared warmth against the cold backdrop of the Orion arm.

Elizabeth kept to her rounds, knowing that staring at a progress bar wouldn't make photons travel any faster. She stripped and ultrasonically cleaned the primary air-recirculation valves in Habitat Ring B, harvested a tray of lipid-rich Chlorella algae from the glowing green vats in hydroponics, and finally coaxed her antique kettle into heating a bulb of spiced tea. Yet every time she passed through the central hub, her eyes were drawn to the secondary holo-tank, where Logos was patiently stacking light curves and stripping away cosmic-ray artifacts.

On the third day, she was tethered loosely to the main console, nursing a fresh bulb of dark tea through a sip-valve while reviewing the anomaly's curiously high radar albedo, when the holo-display suddenly reconfigured itself. The ambient lighting in the module automatically dimmed by ten percent to sharpen contrast—an automated habit Logos only triggered when presenting high-confidence anomalies.

When the AI spoke, its synthesized tenor was flat, stripped of all conversational warmth. It was the closest a shackled machine could come to sounding rattled.

"Dr. Shaw. I have resolved the transverse parallax across a seventy-two-hour baseline and finalized the three-dimensional velocity vector for Visitor Alpha."

Elizabeth lowered her tea bulb, her eyes narrowing at the cascading columns of green telemetry. "Let's hear it."

"My initial radial ping severely underestimated its kinetic state due to the near-head-on approach geometry," Logos said. "Corrected for vector angle, the object is currently traversing the outer Oort shell at 158.4 km/s. That makes it, by a margin of forty-one percent, the fastest extrasolar body ever recorded in the Tharsis catalog."

Elizabeth let out a low whistle. "One hundred and fifty-eight. That’s moving, even for an ejection event. Did you trace the backward asymptote?"

"I have projected its trajectory back across the local bubble," Logos replied. A thin crimson thread lanced through the holographic star map, spearing outward toward the dense molecular clouds of the Orion sector. "It appears to have emerged from the general vector of the Orion Complex. However, at that velocity, and given the cumulative gravitational perturbations of intervening stellar masses over tens of thousands of years, pinpointing an exact star of origin is beyond the resolution of our suite."

"Still, a gravitational slingshot out of a trinary system or a core-collapse supernova kick could account for the speed," Elizabeth mused aloud, her scientist’s brain immediately reaching for conservative, natural mechanisms. "Mars will definitely want the raw tracking files on this one, if only to model the ejection physics. Let's keep the dish on it for another ninety-six hours before we swing back to the M31 radio burst survey. I noticed a periodicity in those Andromeda—"

"That is not the primary anomaly in the dataset, Dr. Shaw."

Elizabeth froze. Her hand hovered inches from the console.

Logos had interrupted her. In fifty-eight years of continuous cohabitation, through equipment failures, solar storms, and bouts of cabin fever, the AI had never once cut across her speech. Its conversational subroutines were hard-coded for deferential turn-taking unless a priority-zero override was triggered.

Slowly, she turned her head toward the central optical lens on the bulkhead. "You just cut me off, Logos."

"Apologies, Custodian. My heuristic weights judged the following telemetry to supersede standard conversational courtesy." The AI paused for a fraction of a second. "The object is decelerating."

Elizabeth blinked. A laugh almost escaped her throat, sharp and incredulous. "Decelerating? Logos, check your sign conventions. At this distance, it’s finally biting into the outer slope of Sol’s gravity well. Even if the pull is micro-newtons, it should be accelerating inward."

"It is not accelerating, Elizabeth," Logos said. "It is decelerating. Rapidly."

Her fingers went slack. The bulb of tea drifted free from her hand, turning lazily in the air between her face and the glowing star chart, a tiny sphere of amber liquid suspended in the blue light.

Rapidly.

"Define 'rapidly,'" she whispered.

"Rapidly by the standards of unpowered celestial mechanics," the AI clarified. "Over the seventy-two-hour high-resolution window, Visitor Alpha has not gained velocity in accordance with solar gravitational attraction. Instead, its forward velocity has decreased by 0.021 m/s. The rate of negative acceleration is constant to within nine decimal places."

Elizabeth didn't breathe. She reached out, shoved the floating tea bulb out of her line of sight, and pulled the raw interferometry spreadsheets straight into her primary field of view. Numbers cascaded in luminous waterfalls. Her mind, honed by a century of orbital mechanics, tore through the differential equations by instinct. Logos did not make arithmetic errors, which meant the error had to lie in the assumptions.

"Asymmetric outgassing," she snapped, her voice suddenly sharp, professional, and cold. "A subsurface pocket of volatile nitrogen or carbon monoxide sublimating on the sunward face, acting as a natural thruster. Run a spectral check for a coma or gas jet."

"Zero volatile emissions detected across infrared, optical, or ultraviolet bands," Logos responded instantly, flagging the flat spectral lines in red.

"Yarkovsky effect? Anisotropic thermal radiation from a fast-spinning, dark body?"

"Insufficient solar flux at zero-point-nine light-years by six orders of magnitude. Furthermore, radar polarimetry indicates the object is not tumbling. Its orientation relative to its velocity vector is locked."

"Solar wind drag? A localized magnetic bow-shock anomaly? Dust-lane friction? Sensor mirror thermal flexure?"

"I have modeled and subtracted all known baryonic, radiative, and instrumental interferences, Dr. Shaw," Logos said, and the sheer, quiet finality in its voice sent a needle of ice straight down Elizabeth’s spine. "None account for more than zero-point-zero-zero-one percent of the observed delta-v. The deceleration is non-Newtonian. Furthermore, the vector of thrust is not random. It is actively trimming its hyperbolic inclination."

A new trajectory line bloomed on the holo-map—a long, graceful, mathematically precise curve that bent the crimson line away from the outer planets and drew it straight toward the heart of the solar system.

"If this exact deceleration profile is maintained," Logos said into the suffocating quiet of the module, "the object will bleed off its interstellar velocity and achieve a zero-relative-velocity parking orbit at the First Lagrangian Point between Earth and Sol. Estimated time to arrival at L1 is six thousand, four hundred and twelve Galactic Standard Years."

Elizabeth stared at the glowing green dot of Earth, nestled close to the blinding pinprick of the Sun, and the crimson line terminating squarely on its doorstep.

"Could there be..." Her voice cracked. She cleared her throat, gripping the edge of the console so hard her knuckles turned white against the dark anodized metal. "Could there be any natural astrophysical mechanism we haven't accounted for? Dark matter clumping? A primordial micro-singularity?"

"Unknown," Logos replied. "However, given the estimated mass derived from its radar cross-section, the kinetic energy being dissipated by this braking maneuver is... profound. I have executed forty-seven thousand Monte Carlo simulations over the last fourteen seconds and cross-verified the health of every optical, thermal, and gravimetric sensor on this station. To a statistical confidence of ninety-nine point nine-nine-nine-eight percent, Dr. Shaw, this detection is real. Something out there is braking for Earth."

Real.

For fifty-eight years she had listened to the cemetery of the galaxy, waiting for a whisper. She had imagined a mathematical primer on the twenty-one-centimeter hydrogen band, a distant beacon pulsing primes from across a spiral arm. She had never once imagined a silent, dark mass screaming out of the Orion sector at a hundred and fifty-eight kilometers a second, riding a brake-burn programmed six millennia in advance.

"Logos," she said, kicking violently off the footrest and catching a handhold near the auxiliary server racks, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. "Bring the tertiary optical backups online. Dump the M31 radio buffers—I want every gigawatt of processing power and every square centimeter of aperture on this station locked onto Visitor Alpha. Start a continuous, raw-state quarantine backup on physical crystal. And pull up the Level-Omega priority transmission cipher for the Tharsis Directorate."

"Level-Omega template initialized, Custodian," Logos confirmed, a blood-red border framing the main communications console. "Standing Mandate protocols for confirmed non-human technological contact require immediate tight-beam laser transmission to Olympus Mons Relay."

"Wait," Elizabeth said.

She let go of the handhold, floating completely free in the center of the cabin, her arms drifting slightly outward as the adrenaline cooled into a heavy, calculating dread. She stared at the blinking red cipher prompt.

"Hold the transmission, Logos. Do not open the laser link."

"Please clarify, Dr. Shaw," the AI said. "The telemetry satisfies all seven statutory thresholds for a Level-Omega alert under the Armstrong Accords."

"And what happens nine months from today when that laser hits the receivers on Mars?" Elizabeth countered, her voice tight and low in the quiet room. "Think it through, Logos. A three-thousand-year-old Tin Can with a six-hundred-year-old sensor spine—crewed by a one-hundred-and-eight-year-old woman whom half the Directorate already considers a bitter crank—blasts an uncorroborated panic code into the heart of the Core Systems. Telling them an alien starship is braking for Earth, citing a two-centimeter-per-second velocity shave that no inner-system telescope will be able to resolve for another four centuries!"

She pulled her knees slightly toward her chest, her mind racing through the political realities of the Solar Mandate.

"Best-case scenario? Aris Thorne and Tuan write it off as senile apophenia combined with quantum jitter on a decaying array, decommission Station 734 on the spot, and shut down the only eyes close enough to watch it," she said. "Worst-case scenario? They believe me. And we plunge ten billion people into a six-thousand-year existential panic over a ghost in a spreadsheet. Helios Dynamics declares a system-wide security emergency, militarizes the outer belt, and suspends civil charters—all on the word of a single unverified sensor log."

She rubbed her temples, feeling the immense, crushing isolation of her post as a physical weight for the first time in her life. Nine light-months of vacuum separated her from another human mind. There was no colleague to look over her shoulder, no second observatory to run an independent confirmation pass. If she cried wolf across the void, she only got one shot.

She needed more than Doppler math. She needed something Aris Thorne couldn't dismiss as a software glitch.

Her eyes snapped back to the crimson trajectory line slicing through the outer Oort Cloud.

"Logos," she said, her voice dropping an octave as a new thought crystallized. "The object is plowing through the local cometary halo at nearly a hundred and sixty kilometers a second. Even in this vacuum, there’s interstellar hydrogen, stray water-ice crystals, and micro-silicates. If it’s expending energy to brake and hitting local dust at that speed... is it ablating?"

A secondary window expanded beside the star map, rendering a high-contrast false-color thermal plume.

"Affirmative, Custodian," Logos said. "High-gain lidar reveals a tenuous, persistent wake of micro-particulates shedding from the object's outer surface, extending approximately forty thousand kilometers behind its current position. A microscopic debris tail."

A fierce, sudden current of hope surged through Elizabeth’s chest. "Physical matter. Can we intercept the wake?"

"The object itself has already passed our optimal delta-v intercept envelope," Logos calculated. "However, the trailing particulate wake is dispersing radially and losing momentum against the interstellar medium. I could reprogram Maintenance Drone Gamma-3 for a high-burn sprint. Its forward micrometeoroid shield is lined with low-density silica aerogel designed to trap high-velocity cometary dust without vaporizing the grains."

"How long to reach the wake and get the shield back to our airlock?"

"At maximum sustainable ion thrust, exhausting ninety-four percent of its reaction mass... three weeks, two days to reach the outer boundary of the debris trail," Logos replied. "And approximately three weeks, four days for the low-energy ballistic return."

Six weeks. Forty-two days of sitting in the dark on a secret that could rewrite the history of the species.

"Don't just send Gamma-3," Elizabeth ordered, pulling herself back to the command console with sharp, decisive economy. "Flush the bays. Send Gamma-3, Gamma-4, and the heavy hull-welding unit, Gamma-6. Stagger their intercept vectors across three different cross-sections of the tail. If one takes a stray ice grain at high speed, I want redundancy."

"Acknowledged. Uploading intercept navigation profiles and venting Drone Bays Three, Four, and Six."

Through the deck plates, Elizabeth felt the faint, triple thump-thump-thump of the magnetic catapults firing the small utility craft out into the black.

She watched their blue transponder pips flare to life on the local display, arcing away from the station like three tiny harpoons cast after a leviathan. Then she turned back to the blinking red Level-Omega screen. She couldn't stay completely silent; if something happened to the station while the drones were out, there had to be a breadcrumb trail in the Tharsis archives. Wait too long, and she was negligent. Scream too loud, and she was mad.

"Logos, purge the Level-Omega cipher," she said quietly. "Open a standard, low-priority administrative form. Bundle it into the routine weekly telemetry dump."

She pulled the holographic keyboard toward her and began to type manually, her aged fingers steady as she buried the greatest discovery in human history beneath layers of dry, self-deprecating academic caution:

Log Entry: Weekly Observational Missive

ADDENDUM TO WEEKLY SECTOR LOG 2850.5.14: Flagging an uncatalogued hyperbolic interstellar body (provisional tag: Visitor Alpha) traversing Sector 4-Delta at ~158 km/s. Preliminary Doppler residuals suggest a minor, non-Newtonian negative acceleration (-0.021 m/s over 72h). Given the age of Spine B's cryo-coolers, this reading is highly consistent with systematic calibration drift or unresolved thermal jitter. Before logging a formal anomaly ticket, I have dispatched three maintenance drones to sweep the object's cometary dust wake to verify standard silicate/ice composition and rule out asymmetric outgassing. Will submit calibration corrections upon drone recovery in ~6 weeks.

— E. Shaw, Custodian.

She read it twice, then hit the physical commit key.

On the status board, the file compressed and dropped into the station’s store-and-forward transmission buffer, waiting for the automated high-gain dish to align with the Mars relay window. Once spat into the dark, that tight-beam pulse of photons would begin its lonely, nine-month crawl inward toward the sun—a message in a bottle that was completely factual, boring enough to be rubber-stamped by a sleepy committee, and the only insurance policy she had for the six agonizing weeks of silence that lay ahead.
