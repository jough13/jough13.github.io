Chapter Two: Port Nowhere

LOCATION: Port Nowhere, Outer Orion Sector (LDN 1622 Rim)
DATE: ~GSY 2851 (Local Subjective Time)

Archivist's Note

Due to this habitat's distance of approximately 450 light-years from Sol, objective electromagnetic confirmation of these events will not reach the Core Systems until GSY ~3301. The "present" of this record exists solely within the protagonist's local light-cone.

The air inside the Stygian Catacombs Bar and Lounge reeked of burnt copper and boiled “peanuts” on the best of days. Four centuries of unwashed regret will do that to a place.

The smell hung in the low-ceilinged cavern in visible strata, a soup of flat synthetic malt, rancid cooking tallow drifting in from the skewer stalls along the concourse, and the tang of ancient atmospheric scrubbers losing their war against hundreds of thousands of desperate lungs. And the smell was just one of the sensory assaults. Beneath every clink of glassware and muttered curse rode the baseline migraine of Port Nowhere itself: the slightly off-kilter, sixty-hertz shudder of the asteroid-station’s thrusters fighting to keep four billion tons of hollowed-out iron rotating at a passable eight-tenths of a G.

Jax sat hunched in a corner booth, his spine flat against the sweating basalt bulkhead, nursing two fingers of something amber and allegedly distilled from grain.

When he tilted the scarred tumbler to his lips, the liquor didn't slosh level. Instead it pulled subtly to the left against the rim, tugged sideways by the phantom of the station's spin. It still felt a bit unnatural, even after all these years, though only an inner-system tourist looked down at a poured drink on Port Nowhere. A local swallowed it before his inner ear could reconcile the geometry.

On the knife-scored laminate of the table between his elbows, his data-slate cast a cyan square upward. Two lines of text blinked in the center:

[ESCROW & OPERATING BALANCE: 18,420.00 CR]
[NOTICE: PORT AUTHORITY LIEN PENDING — BERTH 44-SUB-C]

To a dockside stevedore living in the lower warrens, eighteen thousand credits was a tidy sum—enough to rent a three-meter sleeping tube and eat decent noodles for three standard years. To an independent captain trying to keep an interplanetary hull flying on the Fringe, it was functionally zero.

Eighteen thousand credits wouldn't buy a twenty-percent deuterium top-off at the fuel depot. It wouldn't cover the twenty-six-thousand-credit dockside price of a refurbished capacitor to replace the half-melted unit currently keeping the Void Mite’s carbon scrubbers from poisoning him in his sleep. And it was a drop in the bucket against the four hundred and eighty thousand credits of principal and compounding hourly vigorish he owed Dimitri Volkov.

Worse, at midnight station time—less than ninety minutes from now—the seventy-two-hour grace period on his mooring would expire. And the Void Mite wasn’t exactly unknown to the authorities. The moment the clock rolled over, Port Nowhere’s dockmaster would drop a magnetic impound collar across the Void Mite’s umbilical ring and hand the title deed straight to Volkov.

As long as Jax held the keys to the Void Mite, he owned a fusion bottle, a meter of radiation shielding, and a salvaged Mandate Navy deep-space interferometer array welded to his bow that was worth a hundred thousand credits on the black market. Without a ship, he was excess meat waiting to be monetized, one way or another. The second that impound lock clicked shut, he was a debtor with functional limbs, a moderately expensive cybernetic left eye, and two mostly working kidneys.

Jax stared at the digits until they blurred, tapping at his glass with a calloused thumb. Finally, the banking ledger minimized. In its place, a holo-frame flickered to life, missing every fourth scan-line where a stray voltage spike or cosmic photon had scrambled the internals.

A woman looked up at him through the static: dark hair escaping a grease-stained mechanic’s bandana, a smudge of carbon streaked across the bridge of her nose, eyes crinkling with a fierce, reckless light he hadn't seen in the universe since the day the rock fell.

Ria.

The frame had been captured in the small cargo hold of the Void Mite some six subjective years ago, right after they had finished welding the salvaged Mandate cutter prow onto the old tug’s nose. Back when the ship had been theirs, not his. In Earth standard years, Jax was biologically thirty-nine; on the Mandate’s calendar, he was pushing ninety-six, having slept away nearly six decades in coffins and short relativistic burns bouncing across the Orion Spur. You learned not to get attached to faces when every long jump aged the people you left behind into strangers or ghosts. Ria had been the one exception. She had been supposed to ride in the seat next to him until long after the stars went cold.

"Stop looking at the debt schedule like it’s a hull breach, Jax," he could almost hear her saying over the memory of the drive hum. "One clean strike in the Hesperia belt. High-grade platinum-group core. We pull the ore, we pay off Volkov’s lien on the prow, we buy our registry clean. A fresh start."

Jax tapped the screen dark. The familiar hook behind his breastbone gave its usual twist. Hesperia Minerals hadn't been a strike; it had been a hollowed-out rubble-pile three light-hours out from Port Nowhere with a falsified geological assay, a shear-fault waiting for a thermal drill, and thirty seconds of static-filled screaming over a helmet comm that still woke him up choking some nights.

Jax kept his hand wrapped around his glass and his eyes locked on the beaded curtain of the cantina’s main archway.

"Your dermal conductivity indicates a forty-two percent spike in serum cortisol, Captain," a spry voice murmured from the bone-conduction comm-cuff strapped to his wrist. "Coupled with a resting tachycardia of one hundred and eighteen beats per minute, my heuristics suggest you are either prodromal for a myocardial infarction or preparing to make another catastrophic financial decision."

"Stow it, Mite," Jax rasped, knocking back half of what remained in the tumbler. The alcohol hit the back of his throat like cheap battery acid. "Give me some good news for a change. Tell me you found a backdoor in the berth’s clamp controller or something."

"I have probed the Harbor Authority sub-net three thousand, seven hundred and twenty-two times during the current station rotation cycle," the AI replied, its tone possessing the crisp, clipped diction of an obsolete Core-world navigational tutor whose personality weights had developed a permanent warp over decades of missed maintenance. "While I had successfully delayed the automated lien-flag by feeding the dockmaster's server a recursive customs-manifest loop, a physical security detail has just been dispatched to Pier Four to manually bolt the magnetic boot onto our collar. Furthermore, our maneuvering tanks sit at fourteen percent capacity—sufficient for approximately ten hours of main-drive burn—and our primary life-support capacitor will experience dielectric failure in roughly ninety-seven hours. The probability of the Void Mite departing Port Nowhere and arriving safely elsewhere without an immediate capital injection is 0.3%."

Jax swirled the last amber drops around the bottom of his cup. "Three tenths of a percent. Hell, Mite. Last week we were at zero flat. We're trending up."

The Hesperia claim, or lack thereof, had been Volkov’s plan from the start. The syndicate boss didn't lend money to independent prospectors expecting them to strike platinum; he lent to them so he could foreclose on their hulls for pennies on the dollar. Jax had spent years grinding through the Outer Rim debris shoals since Ria died, running sub-light wreck-dives within a few light-hours of Port Nowhere just to keep up with the hourly interest, watching the door slowly swing shut behind him.

Two men stepped through the beaded archway of the Stygian Catacombs, and Jax felt his heart skip several beats.

They weren't dockside bruisers in standard Port Nowhere attire. Volkov’s auditors never were. They wore charcoal-gray corporate suits tailored for inner-ring spin, moving with the economy of men who had paid a fortune to have their inner ears surgically re-leveled for low-G. Neither carried official station authority, but the crowd of cargo-runners and asteroid miners parted before them like butter before a hot blade. Beneath their brows, identical pairs of aftermarket targeting optics glowed a dull crimson, sweeping the tavern in synchronized facial-recognition arcs.

Jax slid lower into his booth, tilting his chin down so the grease-stained collar of his flight jacket shadowed his face. They knew the impound crew was already on its way to Berth 44 and were now checking his favorite watering holes to collect him once the ship was booted.

He didn't breathe. He didn't look directly at them; rumor had it modern high-end threat-assessment wetware could register the tension of a human stare across a crowded room. Through the gap between two standing Hauler-Clan riggers, he watched the crimson lenses pan across the mid-floor tables, pause for half a heartbeat on his shadowed corner, and then slide past. Maybe they saw him. Maybe the electromagnetic hash from the kitchen’s fryers had baffled their sweep. Without a word, the auditors strode past the bar and vanished behind the soundproof curtain of the VIP alcoves in the rear.

Jax let out a ragged sigh. His grace period was now measured in the time it took two syndicate collectors to talk to the bartenders. He had to get out of there.

Except he couldn't. He couldn’t move from this booth until his lottery ticket showed up.

Jax was gambling his final eighteen thousand credits and his last twenty minutes of freedom on Glitch. Out on the Fringe, where relativistic transit chewed through lifetimes and turned best friends into faded memories, no broker stuck around in the same place for a century. On Port Nowhere, Glitch wasn't a person; Glitch was an inherited role. A hood, an encrypted ledger, and a pitch-scrambled puck passed down through generations of deep-net bottom-feeders who scraped telemetry off dead corporate probes, drifting black-box beacons, and pirated relay buoy cores. Jax had bought salvage coordinates from Glitch four times over the last forty years, and he was fairly sure he had never dealt with the same body twice. He didn't care who was under the rags tonight—human, splice, or something stranger—only that they didn't sell him junk coordinates, and wouldn't hand him over to Volkov.

Volkov was always keen on information he could extract with a bone-saw.

Across the smoky width of the cantina, the night’s commerce ground on, indifferent to one more salvage captain going under. Two booths over, a Xylossian commodities broker towered above a pair of sweating human ice-miners. The alien’s chitinous torso was draped in vacuum-silk, its five compound eyes glittering like wet anthracite while all four of its multi-jointed hands danced across three separate data-slates simultaneously, shaving fractions of a credit off their oxygen futures in silence. Further down the rail, a tall, slender Kiri stood among a rowdy crew of a Hauler-Clan, her translucent skin rippling with slow, rhythmic waves of warm amber bioluminescence—the unmistakable Kiri chord for shared, unguarded mirth.

Jax looked away from the Kiri. It hurt the eyes, seeing something resembling joy in a pit like this.

Just then, a shadow detached itself from the service corridor beside the restrooms and slid without a footstep onto the torn vinyl bench directly opposite him.

Jax’s hand dropped instinctively toward the cut-down plasma torch holstered against his thigh before he caught himself.

The figure across the table was smaller than the last Glitch Jax had dealt with, swaddled from crown to boots in layers of heavy, bleached cloth coated in dust. Beneath the cowl, there was no face, just the snout of a rebreather mask and the rhythmic, wet hiss-click of an internal oxygen concentrator cycling every four seconds.

"You're twenty minutes late," Jax growled. "Volkov’s hounds just walked into the back room."

"Time is a local variable, Jax," Glitch replied. "Plus, I had to wait for the harbor patrol to change shifts on Level Four." The voice didn't come from the mask's voder; it buzzed directly through the flat puck laid on the table between them, scrubbed through so many pitch-randomizers that it sounded like three dead people speaking at once. "Do you have the eighteen thousand?"

"Eighteen flat," Jax said, nudging his slate three inches across the sticky laminate. "Which leaves me four hundred and twenty credits to my name and an empty fuel tank. Before I thumb this transfer and beggar myself, explain to me again why a broker who sells to cartel bosses is sitting in a dive bar offering me Tier-Nine corporate salvage for eighteen grand."

A three-fingered glove slid from the folds of the cloak and rested over the edge of the slate like a dark spider.

"Because on the corporate exchange, this packet isn't worth eighteen thousand; it's worth eighteen million. And anyone who broadcasts it on the local net will be dead before the bidding closes," the puck buzzed. "Eleven standard years ago, a Helios Dynamics deep-reconnaissance corvette, the HD-Perseus, vanished just beyond the outer dust curtain. Before she broke up, she kicked a high-velocity automated black-box courier pod back out of the cloud. That pod spent the next decade crawling blindly through the soot at a few dozen kilometers a second. Fourteen hours ago, it cleared the interference wall, fired a dying burst-squawk, and landed straight in one of our outer-belt magnetic snare nets. We didn’t know what we had until we cracked it, but now it’s confirmed. A Class-Three Precursor emitter. Verified untouched."

Jax’s eyes narrowed, the servos of his cybernetic left optic clicking softly as it focused on the lenses of Glitch’s mask. "If the Perseus went dark eleven years ago, why the sudden fire sale? Sit on the data for a month and sell it to a Hauler-Clan syndicate."

"Three reasons," Glitch hissed. "First: when the Perseus missed her scheduled relativistic return to the Helios regional hub six years back, the Board didn't shrug and write off a trillion-credit survey hull. They dispatched a recovery task force. Three hours ago, Port Nowhere’s outer-perimeter scopes picked up the deceleration flare of a Helios Special Asset picket frigate dropping out of high-velocity at the edge of this system. They're here to sweep the debris shoals for the black box—and I need eighteen thousand in untraceable escrow within the hour to buy a cold-sleep berth on a Xylossian ore-barge before they lock down the port."

Jax felt the hairs on the back of his neck prickle. That was how corporate war worked out in the Fringe: slow, patient, and inescapable, a hammer raised a decade ago finally coming down tonight.

"Second, and this is why I thought you would be an interested buyer," Glitch continued, "the signal inside the cloud isn't electromagnetic. Light and radar die a hundred meters past the soot wall. It’s a modulated neutrino cadence, and to read a neutrino wake without getting torn apart by gravimetric shear, a ship needs a near-military-grade forward interferometer. Like the Mandate Navy cutter prow welded to the nose of the Void Mite."

Jax caught his breath. The cowled head leaned forward two inches.

"And third," Glitch spoke, the modulated voice now barely a whisper, "because no captain on Port Nowhere who isn't already a dead man would be lunatic enough to drop into the heart of LDN 1622."

The alcohol in Jax’s stomach turned to lead.

He knew the cloud. Every spacer moored at Port Nowhere lived in its shadow. Centuries ago, the leeward shoals of the nebula had sparked a boom, drawing waves of prospectors chasing heavy-metal asteroids and platinum-rich core fragments flung loose from the dark. That rush built Port Nowhere. Over the generations, though, corporate dredgers and wildcat crews stripped the surrounding belts down to barren slag until all the low-hanging fruit was gone. Now the hollowed-out rock survived on stubbornness and geography. It sat at a useful crossroads between outer-sector trade lanes, and the docks, spin-rings, and brokerages were already bolted into the iron. Too tapped out to thrive and too established to abandon, it had settled into a rusted-out waystation for haulers, smugglers, and anyone running out of road.

Just past the outer mooring buoys hung the dark wall that had started it all. On the Fringe, they called LDN 1622 the Shipbreaker’s Graveyard: a hundred-light-year snarl of hyper-dense molecular hydrogen, carbon-soot curtains, protostellar jets, and gravitational shear-zones where failed brown dwarfs and wandering mass-shadows tore ship hulls apart like wet tissue. Standard radar bounced off the dust walls like a flashlight off a mirror; optical scopes went blind; fusion intakes choked on the heavy gas. Rumor still insisted the deep interior hid untouched ore veins and some even whispered of Precursor salvage. Once a generation some desperate crew limped back out rich enough to vanish forever. Most just vanished. Sane captains traded their manifests at Port Nowhere, topped off their water tanks, and left the Graveyard to the dead.

"That's not a salvage tip, Glitch," Jax said, his voice dropping to a flat rasp. "With fourteen percent in my deuterium tanks, I wouldn't even have the delta-v to brake once I crossed the soot line. That's a one-way ticket to oblivion."

"Then stay here and keep your eighteen thousand," the induction speaker buzzed indifferently. "A Harbor Authority deck-gang is currently halfway down Concourse C with a hydraulic impound clamp for Berth 44, and Volkov’s two surgical specialists in the back room just finished their kaf. When they walk out that curtain in ninety seconds, they are going to paralyze you from the third vertebra down, sign the foreclosure papers on the Void Mite with your own thumbprint, and carry you to the chop-docks in a cargo tarp. I hear Dimitri has a Xylossian cyber-broker docked on Pier Nine looking for a vintage Mandate recon optic. They won't even waste local anesthetic when they spoon that left eye out of your skull."

Beneath the table, Jax’s fingers twitched. Without meaning to, he raised his hand and brushed the cold, matte-black titanium housing fused into his left orbital socket—a piece of black-market military hardware he’d won off a recon officer in a high-stakes poker pot thirty calendar years ago, back when he still had luck on his side.

He looked from the heavy curtain at the back of the cantina to the hooded wraith across the booth.

If he stayed on Port Nowhere, he lost the Mite in twenty minutes and his life by morning. If he bought the file and blew the berth clamps, he was throwing an unrefueled tugboat into the worst navigational hazard in the sector on the gamble that some ancient god-machine was out there, pulsing in the dark with something worth stealing.

A ninety-nine percent chance of freezing to death in an unmapped dark cloud versus a one hundred percent chance of dying strapped to Volkov's table.

"Drop the packet," Jax growled, and pressed his thumb hard against the slate's biometric transfer pad.

The credit display flashed once: [AVAILABLE BALANCE: 420.00 CR]. Simultaneously, an encrypted data-icon materialized in his local storage buffer.

Glitch’s three-fingered glove vanished back into the dusty robes. The broker was already sliding out of the booth with that eerie, boneless silence. "Pleasure doing business, Captain. Do try to beat the actuarial tables. Whoever inherits this hood after me will appreciate the repeat business."

Before Jax could answer, a group of drunken ore-haulers stumbled between their table and the aisle; when they cleared a second later, the bench opposite him was empty, and the rear VIP curtain was just beginning to part.

Jax didn't finish the dregs of his glass. He snatched up his slate, pulled his collar high, and slipped out through the side kitchen door into the noisy, steam-choked bowels of the Lower Concourse.

Then, he ran.

Through the curved, claustrophobic arteries of Port Nowhere, his boots rang against iron deck-gratings slick with condensation. Because the habitat had been hollowed out of an irregular asteroid over generations of lawless expansion, the corridors didn't follow sane architectural rules; they twisted along ancient veins, climbing inward toward the spin axis where the artificial gravity bled away from a heavy trudge to a long, bounding half-float. Flickering neon ideograms in Mandarin, Anglic, and Xylossian trade-glyphs blurred past above open-flame noodle kiosks, unlicensed cyber-clinics, and pawn cages stacked with patched EVA suits.

"Captain," Mite’s voice crackled urgently through his wrist-cuff as Jax vaulted a stairwell railing in quarter-gravity, dropping twenty feet down a radial access shaft toward the outer docking ring. "Harbor Security Team Four has just arrived at the outer bulkhead of Berth 44-Sub-C. They have connected a hydraulic power-pack to the umbilical collar and are initializing the Stage-One magnetic impound clamp. Estimated time to physical lock-down is three minutes and forty seconds."

"Don't wait for me to get in the chair, Mite!" Jax panted, his lungs burning as he hit the spin-ward deck at a dead sprint. "Start hot-prepping the primary fusion bottle right now! Bypass the pre-ignition checks!"

"Bypassing pre-ignition checks on an eighty-year-old magnetic-pinch coil carries a fourteen percent risk of plasma back-flash," Mite noted primly. "However, given that the alternative is becoming the property of Mr. Volkov’s scrap-metal division, I am bridging the safeties now."

Jax rounded the final dog-leg of Sub-Corridor C and skidded to a halt behind a stacked pallet of deuterium flasks.

Thirty meters ahead, at the end of the narrow jetty, the airlock of Berth 44-Sub-C was bathed in the orange glare of a Port Authority seizure beacon. Two dock-enforcers in yellow exo-frames were crouched beside the boarding collar, wrestling a massive, C-shaped locking boot over the Void Mite’s main umbilical flange, while a third guard stood by the wall terminal with an arc-welder, waiting to spot-weld the release shut.

Through the thick quartz window of the jetty, Jax could see the nose of his ship.

Even in his most sentimental moments, he couldn't have called the Void Mite pretty. She had started life a century ago as a heavy-duty ice-tug before passing through the hands of three bankrupt prospectors, two blockade runners, and finally Jax and Ria. Her blunt, slab-sided hull was dominated by the oversized Mandate Navy sensor prow they had grafted onto her bow, making her look like a rusted bulldog with a bayonet strapped to her snout. The starboard thruster pod was three shades darker than the port, patched with mismatched hexagonal tiles of ablative ceramic scavenged from a half-dozen bone-yards. She was ugly, she was cranky, and she was held together by equal parts love and spite.

She was also the last piece of Ria still breathing in the universe, and nobody was putting a chain on her while Jax had two hands.

"Mite," Jax whispered, drawing the plasma torch from his thigh holster and thumbing the regulator past the safety stop. "Can you vent the jetty’s fire-suppression line into this corridor?"

"Technically, tampering with municipal safety infrastructure carries a five-hundred-credit fine," Mite replied. "But that feels irrelevant to our current situation. Venting line four... now."

Above the heads of the three dock-enforcers, a high-pressure valve in the ceiling shrieked and blew its gasket.

Instantly, a blizzard of freezing nitrogen vapor erupted downward into the jetty, dropping visibility to zero and flash-freezing the condensation on the floor into a sheet of ice.

The guard with the arc-welder yelled in surprise, stumbling backward on the slick grating. Jax was already moving.

Launching himself off the cargo pallet in the low peripheral gravity, Jax sailed a dozen meters through the white fog, his left optic switching to infrared. The three men glowed like orange silhouettes in the mist. Jax hit the deck plates boots-first between the two exo-riggers and jammed the white-hot jet of his overcharged plasma torch straight through the main hydraulic feed-hose of the impound clamp.

Boiling red hydraulic fluid sprayed into the nitrogen mist, flashing into a pink cloud of ice crystals as the clamp's jaws seized half-closed.

"Hey—!" the lead rigger roared, swinging a pneumatic wrench the size of a fire hydrant at Jax’s head.

Jax ducked under the whistling iron arm, planted his boot against the off-balanced rigger’s knee to send the top-heavy exo-frame crashing onto the ice, and slapped his palm against the Void Mite’s outer airlock pad.

The hatch snapped open. Jax dove headfirst into the boarding tube, hit the inner emergency-cycle switch with his elbow, and listened to the outer door slam shut a fraction of a second before a pneumatic rivet-gun hammered three dents into the exterior.

He didn't pause to catch his breath. Hauling himself hand-over-hand down the three-meter access spine into the cramped, oil-scented cylinder of the flight deck, he slid into the worn leather pilot’s couch, yanked the five-point crash webbing tight across his chest, and jammed his data-slate into the console.

"Start cracking Glitch's packet," Jax barked, his fingers already flying across the overhead breaker panel as the ship’s ancient fusion reactor roared to a shaky idle behind the aft bulkhead. "And arm the emergency docking collar charges. They’ve got the outer ring half-booted. If we wait for the cycle, they’ll weld us to the rock."

"Decrypting now, Captain," Mite replied from the cockpit speakers, the tiny, pea-sized sphere of cyan light materializing above the throttle quadrant. "And the auxiliary bus is routed to the shear-bolts. For the record, detonating an explosive disconnect inside a pressurized jetty constitutes felony harbor-destruction, assault on municipal officers, and breach of maritime quarantine. You will not be setting foot on Port Nowhere again unless you return rich enough to buy the Port Authority outright, and with a story loud enough to keep Dimitri Volkov from shooting you on the gangway."

Outside the hull, a heavy, metallic THUD-CLANG shuddered through the forward frame as the dock-crew hooked a secondary winch-cable onto the nose ring. A Port Authority klaxon began to wail. Through the frosted bullseye of the forward viewport, the helmet of a dock-enforcer suddenly pressed against the glass, his eyes widening as he saw the Void Mite’s attitude thrusters spitting white pre-ignition frost inside the docking tube.

"I'll make sure to bring back a souvenir," Jax growled, slamming his palm down on the crimson release.

CRACK-THUMP.

Six shaped docking collar charges ringed around the Void Mite’s forward docking flange detonated in sequence. They sheared the titanium umbilical bolts clean in half and blasted the half-clamped hydraulic boot backward into the jetty in a hurricane of decompressing station air, shattered glass, and nitrogen snow.

Driven by the violent pneumatic kick of the breached airlock and four screaming jets of compressed nitrogen, the bulk of the Void Mite shot backward out of the docking trench like a cork popped from a bottle.

"Harbor defense batteries on Pier Four and Pier Six are tracking us," Mite reported as the ship tumbled into the floodlights of the outer anchorage. Jax wrenched the ship between the rusted hulls of two moored ore-barges while red targeting lasers licked across her mismatched ceramic tiles. "Spin-gravity Coriolis shear is pulling us toward the lower gantry."

"Don't fight the spin. Ride it!" Jax yelled, kicking the starboard yaw pedals hard to the floor.

Instead of climbing into the open radar cone of the station's point-defense turrets, he let the asteroid’s rotational momentum sling the Void Mite downward into the jagged canyon of the equatorial mining trench. For heart-stopping seconds, he flew the seventy-ton tug like a dirt-bike through a drainage ditch, skimming just meters above rusted conveyor towers and silent strip-mining buckets, using four billion tons of spinning nickel-iron to mask his hull from the harbor guns.

Then the ragged lip of the asteroid’s south pole fell away beneath their belly, and the velvet scatter of the Orion Spur opened up ahead.

Jax killed the thrusters and let the tug coast into the station’s radar shadow. The shaking stopped. The screams of the harbor klaxons were long gone, swallowed by vacuum. In the sudden quiet of the cockpit, the only sounds left were Jax’s ragged breathing and the high-pitched whine of cooling fans behind the instrument board.

Behind them, Port Nowhere gradually shrank to a spinning cinder studded with angry yellow lights. Far out on the opposite rim of the system, barely a pinpoint on the long-range optical scope, burned the pale blue deceleration plume of a Helios Dynamics picket frigate sliding into the neighborhood. There was no going back to the rock.

Jax held his breath. They could still send someone after him. Would they? Was he worth it? He waited a minute. Two. Ten. No pursuit appeared. He let himself exhale and unbuckled his harness.

"Alright, Mite," Jax said, wiping a bead of sweat from his forehead with the back of a trembling sleeve. "Tell me I didn't just burn my last bridge for eighteen thousand credits' worth of static."

"Polymorphic wrappers bypassed," Mite said. The holo-emitter stuttered, coughed a spark of blue static, and projected a three-dimensional chart into the dark cabin.

It was a high-density gravimetric rendering of the outer shoreline of LDN 1622, a vast, swirling curtain of molecular hydrogen and carbon soot swallowing the stars along the entire starboard horizon. Seventy astronomical units past the lightless dust wall, inside a lethal pocket of colliding gas filaments tagged OMC-1 North, a crimson dot pulsed like a heartbeat.

Beneath the reticle scrolled the fragmented black-box log of the HD-Perseus:

[...SOURCE CONFIRMED ARTIFICIAL... ANOMALOUS NEUTRINO EMISSION... PATTERNED FIVE-PULSE CADENCE, ZERO DECAY... MASS SIGNATURE CONSISTENT WITH CLASS-3 PRECURSOR STRUCTURE... EXTREME GRAVIMETRIC SHEAR IN SECTOR... HULL INTEGRITY FAILING... RECOMMEND IMMEDIATE WITHDRAWAL...]

Beside the text, a waveform graph unfolded, and Jax’s breath caught in his throat.

He leaned forward, the light of the hologram reflecting off the titanium rim of his left optic. His internal lenses zoomed in on the map: five sharp spikes of neutrino flux repeating every four seconds with atomic precision against a background of nebular noise, trailed by a narrow-band gravimetric wake that looked like a river cutting straight through the dust.

"It’s real," Jax whispered, his mouth suddenly bone-dry. "A Class-Three Precursor megastructure. And look at that secondary trace, Mite. The neutrino pulses are carving a laminar shear-channel right through the dust wall. With the Mite’s prow, we can actually read that channel and surf it in."

"Captain, please listen to the mathematics before your desperation overrides your brainstem," Mite said. The trace of dry irony had vanished from the AI’s voice, replaced by something akin to dread. "I am cross-referencing the distance to OMC-1 North against our fourteen-percent fuel reserve and the lifespan of our failing CO2 capacitor. To reach those coordinates on our remaining fuel requires a continuous, ten-hour insertion burn at two-point-eight Gs, consuming ninety-six percent of our reaction mass right at the edge of the dust wall."

"Leaving us four percent for attitude adjustment inside the cloud," Jax said, his eyes locked on the five-beat pulse.

"Leaving us zero percent to brake, reverse course, or escape," Mite countered sharply. "Once the main torch runs dry, we will be an unpowered projectile hurtling into a potential well hurricane at nearly a thousand kilometers per second. Even if our sensor prow allows us to ride the neutrino wake on cold-gas thrusters without being crushed into foil by tidal shears, the ballistic drift across ten and a half billion kilometers of soot will take one hundred and twenty-two days."

"Then we ration the air and stretch the scrubbers."

"We cannot stretch a ruptured capacitor for one hundred and twenty-two days, Jax." The cyan pea flared bright white above the console. "To keep that dielectric core from blowing out before we reach the target, I will have to shut down the galley, kill ninety percent of the avionics, and drop cabin temperature to minus-twelve degrees Celsius for four months. You will be starving in a dark closet in the middle of an unmapped graveyard, praying that an alien ruin nobody has ever seen somehow possesses a pressurized docking bay and compatible fusion fuel. My Monte Carlo simulation places our probability of surviving arrival at less than 1.2%, and our probability of ever returning to human space at a number indistinguishable from zero."

Silence settled over the tiny cockpit.

Jax looked down at the glowing console. Four hundred and twenty credits sat in his escrow account. Behind him lay a harbor where every turret and every syndicate bounty hunter now had his transponder tag burned into memory, and beyond that, a Helios frigate moving in to sweep the lanes clean. He thought of Ria’s laugh frozen behind missing scan-lines, and the years he had spent slowly bleeding to death one dirty scrap-haul at a time while waiting for Volkov to finally take her ship.

A slow, wild, utterly fatalistic grin spread across his face, the expression Ria used to call his dead-man's tell right before he shoved his last stack of chips.

"One point two percent," Jax murmured, his left hand resting on the attitude stick. "Do you have any idea what a single Class-Three Precursor data-core fetches, Mite? If there’s an active installation sitting at the bottom of that well, we don't just pay off Volkov. We buy the dockmaster a new jetty, walk back into the Stygian Catacombs in platinum-plated boots, and tell a story nobody in the Orion Spur can afford to ignore."

"And the ninety-eight-point-eight percent probability that we freeze to death in the dark long before we get there?" Mite asked quietly.

"We’ve been dying by millimeters for years, buddy," Jax said softly. "I’m done dying on the installment plan. If we’re going out, we’re going out swinging at the doorsteps of ancient gods."

For a dozen seconds, the cockpit remained completely still. Then the tiny cyan sphere dimmed to low-power amber. Somewhere beneath the deck plates, the cabin heaters clicked off with a dull, final thunk, and the first thin breath of deep-space cold began to seep through the hull plating.

"Insertion vector for LDN 1622 is locked," Mite announced, resignation thick in its synthesized voice. "Ten hours of main-drive acceleration fuel remaining. Once you advance that lever, there is no turning back."

Ahead of the bow, barely a few light-hours across the outer shoals, hung the lightless wall of the Shipbreaker’s Graveyard, swallowing half the heavens like an ink-black cliff rising out of the dark.

Jax didn't look in the rear camera at the shrinking lights of Port Nowhere. Behind him was nothing but a rigged ledger, a blasted jetty, and a cold slab in the mortuary with his name on it. Ahead waited four months of freezing darkness, a five-beat heartbeat in the abyss, and a one-in-a-hundred shot at everything.

He cinched his crash webbing back across his chest, wrapped his hand around the main fusion throttle, and punched it.
