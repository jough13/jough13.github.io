Chapter Two: Port Nowhere

LOCATION: Port Nowhere, Outer Orion Sector (LDN 1622 Rim)
DATE: ~GSY 2851 (Local Subjective Time)

Archivist's Note

Due to this habitat's distance of approximately 450 light-years from Sol, objective electromagnetic confirmation of these events will not reach the Core Systems until GSY ~3301. The "present" of this record exists solely within the protagonist's local light-cone.

The air inside the Stygian Catacombs Bar and Lounge reeked of burnt copper and boiled “peanuts” on the best of days. Four centuries of unwashed regret will do that to a place.

It hung in the low-ceilinged cavern in visible strata, a soupy emulsion of flat synthetic malt, rancid cooking tallow drifting in from the skewer stalls along the concourse, and the sharp tang of an ancient atmospheric scrubber losing its war against a hundred thousand desperate lungs. Beneath every clink of glassware and muttered curse rode the baseline migraine of Port Nowhere itself: the slightly off-kilter, sixty-hertz shudder of the asteroid-station’s thrusters fighting to keep four billion tons of hollowed-out iron rotating at a passable eight-tenths of a G.

Jax sat hunched in a corner booth, his spine flat against the sweating basalt bulkhead, nursing two fingers of something amber and allegedly distilled from grain.

When he tilted the scarred tumbler to his lips, the liquor didn't slosh level; it pulled subtly to the left against the rim, tugged sideways by the phantom of the station's spin. It still felt a little unnatural, even after all these years, though only an inner-system tourist looked down at a poured drink on Port Nowhere. A local just swallowed it before his inner ear could reconcile the geometry.

On the knife-scored laminate of the table between his elbows, his data-slate cast a cyan square upward across his knuckles. Two lines of text blinked in the center of the display:

[ESCROW & OPERATING BALANCE: 18,420.00 CR]
[NOTICE: PORT AUTHORITY LIEN PENDING — BERTH 44-SUB-C]

Jax stared at the digits until they blurred, then tapped the bruised glass with a calloused thumb. The banking ledger minimized. In its place, a corrupted holo-frame flickered to life, missing every fourth scan-line where a stray voltage spike or cosmic photon had scrambled the internals.

A woman looked up at him through the static: dark hair escaping a grease-stained mechanic’s bandana, a smudge of carbon paste streaked across the bridge of her nose, and eyes crinkling with a fierce, reckless light he hadn't seen in the universe since the day the rock fell.

Ria.

The frame had been captured on the flight deck of the Void Mite six subjective years ago, right after they had finished welding the salvaged Mandate cutter prow onto the old tug’s nose—back when the ship had been theirs, not his. In meat years, Jax was barely thirty-nine; on the Mandate’s calendar, he was pushing ninety-six, having slept away nearly six decades in coffins and relativistic burns bouncing across the Orion Spur. You learned not to get attached to faces when every long jump aged the people you left behind into strangers or ghosts. Ria had been the one exception. She had been supposed to ride in the next berth over until the stars went cold.

"Stop looking at the debt schedule like it’s an airlock breach, Jax," he could almost hear her saying over the memory of the drive hum. "One clean strike in the Hesperia belt. High-grade platinum-group core. We pull the ore, we pay off Volkov’s lien on the prow, we buy our registry clean. A fresh start."

Jax tapped the screen dark. The familiar hook behind his breastbone gave its usual twist. Hesperia Minerals hadn't been a strike; it had been a hollowed-out rubble-pile three light-hours out from Port Nowhere with a falsified geological assay, a shear-fault waiting for a thermal drill, and thirty seconds of static-shredded screaming over a helmet comm that still woke him up choking some nights.

To a dockside stevedore living in the lower warrens, eighteen thousand credits was a tidy sum—enough to rent a three-meter sleeping tube and eat decent noodles for three standard years. To an independent captain trying to keep an interplanetary hull flying on the Fringe, it was functionally zero.

Eighteen thousand credits wouldn't buy a twenty-percent deuterium top-off at the fuel depot. It wouldn't cover the twenty-six-thousand-credit dockside price of a refurbished capacitor to replace the half-melted unit currently keeping the Void Mite’s carbon scrubbers from poisoning him in his sleep. And it was a drop in the bucket against the four hundred and eighty thousand credits of principal and compounding hourly vigorish he owed Dimitri Volkov.

Worse, at midnight station time—less than ninety minutes from now—the seventy-two-hour grace period on his mooring would expire. The moment the clock rolled over, Port Nowhere’s dockmaster would drop a magnetic impound collar across the Void Mite’s umbilical ring and hand the title deed straight to Volkov.

On the Fringe, a man without a ship wasn't a citizen; he was excess rabble waiting to be monetized. As long as Jax held the keys to the Void Mite, he owned a fusion bottle, a meter of radiation shielding, and a salvaged Mandate Navy deep-space interferometer array welded to his bow that was worth a hundred thousand credits on the black market. The second that impound lock clicked shut, he was a crippled debtor with a moderately expensive cybernetic left eye and two mostly working kidneys.

Jax kept his right hand wrapped around his glass and his eyes locked on the beaded curtain of the cantina’s main archway.

"Your dermal conductivity indicates a forty-two percent spike in serum cortisol, Captain," a dry, unhurried voice murmured from the scarred bone-conduction comm-cuff strapped to his left wrist. "Coupled with a resting tachycardia of one hundred and eighteen beats per minute, my medical heuristics suggest you are either prodromal for a myocardial infarction or preparing to make another catastrophic financial decision."

"Stow it, Mite," Jax rasped into his collar, knocking back half of what remained in the tumbler. The alcohol hit the back of his throat like cheap battery acid. Good. Pain meant the nerves were still wired right. "Tell me you found a backdoor in the berth’s clamp controller."

"I have probed the Harbor Authority sub-net three thousand, seven hundred and twenty-two times during the current rotation," the ship’s AI replied, its tone possessing the crisp, clipped diction of an obsolete Core-world navigational tutor whose personality weights had developed a permanent warp over decades of missed maintenance. "While I have successfully delayed the automated lien-flag by feeding the dockmaster's server a recursive customs-manifest loop, a physical security detail has just been dispatched to Pier Four to manually bolt the magnetic boot onto our collar. Furthermore, our maneuvering tanks sit at fourteen percent capacity—sufficient for roughly four hours of main-drive burn—and our primary life-support capacitor will experience dielectric rupture in roughly sixty-seven hours. The statistical probability of the Void Mite departing Port Nowhere without an immediate capital injection is 0.003%."

Jax swirled the last warm drops at the bottom of his cup. "Three thousandths of a percent. Hell, Mite. Last week we were at zero flat. We're trending up."

The Hesperia claim had been Volkov’s slaughterhouse from the start. The syndicate boss didn't lend money to independent prospectors expecting them to strike platinum; he lent them money so he could foreclose on custom hulls for pennies on the dollar. Jax had spent years grinding through the Outer Rim debris shoals since Ria died, running sub-light wreck-dives within a few light-hours of Port Nowhere just to keep up with the hourly interest, watching the door slowly swing shut behind him.

Tonight, the latch clicked.

Two men stepped through the beaded archway of the Stygian Catacombs, and the ambient noise near the door dropped half an octave.

They weren't dockside bruisers in standard thug attire. Volkov’s "auditors" never were. They wore immaculate, charcoal-gray corporate suits tailored for inner-ring spin, moving with the predatory economy of men who had paid a fortune to have their inner ears surgically re-leveled for low-G. Neither carried a visible firearm, yet the crowd of cartel gun-runners and asteroid miners parted before them like butter before a hot blade. Beneath their neat brows, identical pairs of aftermarket targeting optics glowed a dull crimson, sweeping the tavern in synchronized facial-recognition arcs.

Jax slid three inches lower onto his tailbone, tilting his chin down so the grease-stained collar of his flight jacket shadowed his face.

He didn't breathe. He didn't look directly at them; high-end threat-assessment wetware could register the micro-tension of a human stare across a crowded room. Through the gap between two standing Hauler-Clan riggers, he watched the crimson lenses pan across the mid-floor tables, pause for half a heartbeat on his shadowed corner, and then slide past. Either the electromagnetic hash from the kitchen’s fryers had baffled their sweep, or they knew the impound crew was already on its way to Berth 44 and were simply checking his favorite watering holes to collect him once the ship was booted.

Without a word, the auditors glided past the bar and vanished behind the heavy soundproof curtain of the VIP alcoves in the rear.

Jax let out a ragged sigh. His grace period was no longer measured in hours; it was measured in the time it took two syndicate collectors to talk to the bartenders.

Except he couldn't move an inch until his ghost showed up.

He was gambling his final eighteen thousand credits and his last twenty minutes of freedom on Glitch. Out on the Fringe, where relativistic transit chewed through lifetimes and turned old friends into memories, no broker stayed in the same booth for a century. Glitch wasn't a person; Glitch was an inherited seat. A hood, an encrypted ledger, and a pitch-scrambled puck passed down through generations of deep-net bottom-feeders who scraped telemetry off dead corporate probes, drifted black-box beacons, and pirated relay buoys. Jax had bought salvage coordinates from Glitch four times over the last forty calendar years, and he was fairly sure he had never dealt with the same body twice. He didn't care who was under the rags tonight—human, splice, or something stranger—only that the franchise didn't sell bad coordinates and wouldn't hand him over to Volkov.

Volkov never paid for information he could extract with a bone-saw.

Across the smoky width of the cantina, the night’s commerce ground on, indifferent to one more salvage captain going under. Two booths over, a Xylossian commodities broker towered above a pair of sweating human ice-miners; the alien’s chitinous torso was draped in vacuum-silk, its five compound eyes glittering like wet anthracite while all four of its multi-jointed hands danced across three separate data-slates simultaneously, shaving fractions of a credit off their oxygen futures in silence. Further down the brass rail, a tall, slender Kiri stood among a rowdy crew of a Hauler-Clan, her translucent skin rippling with slow, rhythmic waves of warm amber bioluminescence—the unmistakable Kiri chord for shared, unguarded mirth.

Jax looked away from the Kiri. It hurt the eyes, seeing something that clean in a pit like this.

A shadow detached itself from the service corridor beside the restrooms and slid without a footstep onto the torn vinyl bench directly opposite him.

Jax’s hand dropped instinctively toward the cut-down plasma torch holstered against his thigh before he caught himself.

The figure across the table was smaller than the last Glitch Jax had dealt with twelve calendar years ago, swaddled from crown to boots in layers of heavy, radiation-bleached cloth coated in dead asteroid dust. Beneath the cowl, there was no face—only the scarred snout of a rebreather mask and the rhythmic, wet hiss-click of an internal oxygen concentrator cycling every four seconds.

"You're twenty minutes late," Jax growled. "Volkov’s hounds just walked into the back room."

"Time is a local variable, Jax. And I had to wait for the harbor patrol to change shifts on Level Four," Glitch replied. The voice didn't come from the mask's voder; it buzzed directly through a flat induction puck laid on the table between them, scrubbed through so many pitch-randomizers that it sounded like three dead people speaking in unison. "Do you have the eighteen thousand?"

"Eighteen flat," Jax said, nudging his slate three inches across the sticky laminate. "Which leaves me four hundred and twenty credits to my name and an empty fuel tank. Before I thumb this transfer and beggar myself, explain to me again why a broker who usually sells to cartel bosses is sitting in a dive bar offering me a Tier-Nine corporate salvage lead for eighteen grand."

A three-fingered glove—whether tailored for a genetic splice, a mechanical prosthetic, or something stranger, Jax couldn't tell—slid from the folds of the cloak and rested over the edge of the slate like a dark spider.

"Because on the corporate exchange, this packet isn't worth eighteen thousand; it's worth two million. And anyone who broadcasts it on the local net will be dead before the bidding closes," the induction puck buzzed. "Eleven standard years ago, a Helios Dynamics deep-reconnaissance corvette, the HD-Perseus, vanished inside the outer dust curtain. Before her hull broke up, she kicked an automated black-box courier pod backward out of the cloud on cold thrusters. That pod spent over a decade crawling blindly through the soot at a few dozen kilometers a second. Fourteen hours ago, it finally cleared the interference wall, fired a dying burst-squawk, and landed straight in one of our outer-belt snare nets. Confirmed Class-Three Precursor emitter. Untouched."

Jax’s eyes narrowed, the servos of his cybernetic left optic clicking softly as it focused on the lenses of Glitch’s mask. "If the Perseus went dark eleven years ago, why the sudden fire sale? Sit on the data for a month and sell it to a Hauler-Clan syndicate."

"Three reasons," Glitch hissed. "First: when the Perseus missed her scheduled relativistic return to the Helios regional hub six years back, the Board didn't shrug and write off a billion-credit survey hull. They dispatched a recovery task force. Three hours ago, Port Nowhere’s outer-perimeter scopes picked up the deceleration flare of a Helios Special Asset picket frigate dropping out of high-velocity drift at the edge of this system. They're here to sweep the debris shoals for the black box—and I need eighteen thousand in untraceable bearer-escrow within the hour to buy a cold-sleep berth on a Xylossian ore-barge before they lock down the port."

Jax felt the hairs on the back of his neck prickle. That was how corporate war worked without faster-than-light drives: slow, patient, and inescapable, a hammer raised a decade ago finally coming down tonight.

"Second," Glitch continued, "the signal inside the cloud isn't electromagnetic. Light and radar die ten meters past the soot wall. It’s a modulated neutrino cadence, and to read a neutrino wake without getting torn apart by gravimetric shear, a ship needs a military-grade forward interferometer. Like the Mandate Navy cutter prow welded to the nose of the Void Mite."

The cowled head leaned forward two inches.

"And third," Glitch whispered, "because no captain on Port Nowhere who isn't already a dead man would be lunatic enough to drop into the heart of LDN 1622."

The alcohol in Jax’s stomach turned to lead.

He knew the dark cloud; every spacer moored at Port Nowhere lived in its shadow. The station itself existed solely as a ragged frontier anchorage bolted onto the leeward shoals of the nebula—the last rock before the edge of the map. LDN 1622 wasn't a luminous stellar nursery like the Great Nebula to the south. On the Fringe, they called it the Shipbreaker’s Graveyard: a hundred-light-year snarl of hyper-dense molecular hydrogen, carbon-soot curtains, protostellar jets, and gravitational shear-zones where failed brown dwarfs and wandering mass-shadows tore ship hulls apart like wet tissue. Standard radar bounced off the dust walls like a flashlight off a mirror; optical scopes went blind; fusion intakes choked on the heavy gas. Ships didn't come back from the Graveyard with Precursor relics. They didn't come back at all.

"That's not a salvage tip, Glitch," Jax said, his voice dropping to a flat rasp. "With fourteen percent in my deuterium tanks, I wouldn't even have the delta-v to brake once I crossed the soot line. That's a one-way ticket into a meat-grinder."

"Then stay here and keep your eighteen thousand," the induction speaker buzzed indifferently. "A Harbor Authority deck-gang is currently halfway down Concourse C with a hydraulic impound clamp for Berth 44, and Volkov’s two surgical specialists in the back room just finished their kaf. When they walk out that curtain in ninety seconds, they are going to paralyze you from the third vertebra down, sign the foreclosure papers on the Void Mite with your own thumbprint, and carry you to the chop-docks in a cargo tarp. I hear Dimitri has a Xylossian cyber-broker docked on Pier Nine looking for a vintage Mandate recon optic. They won't even waste local anesthetic when they spoon that left eye out of your skull."

Beneath the table, Jax’s fingers twitched. Without meaning to, he raised his hand and brushed the cold, matte-black titanium housing fused into his left orbital socket—a piece of black-market military hardware he’d won off a cashiered recon officer in a high-stakes poker pot thirty calendar years ago, back when he still had luck on his side.

He looked from the heavy velvet curtain at the back of the cantina to the hooded wraith across the booth.

If he stayed on Port Nowhere, he lost Ria’s ship in twenty minutes and his life by morning. If he bought the file and blew the berth clamps, he was throwing an unrefueled tugboat into the worst navigational hazard in the sector on the gamble that whatever ancient god-machine was pulsing in the dark had something worth stealing, and a safe harbor to park in before his air ran out.

A ninety-nine percent chance of freezing to death in an unmapped dark cloud versus a one hundred percent chance of dying strapped to Volkov's table.

"Drop the packet," Jax growled, and pressed his thumb hard against the slate's biometric transfer pad.

The credit display flashed once: [AVAILABLE BALANCE: 420.00 CR]. Simultaneously, an encrypted data-icon materialized in his local storage buffer.

Glitch’s three-fingered glove vanished back into the dusty robes. The broker was already sliding out of the booth with that eerie, boneless silence. "Pleasure doing business, Captain. Do try to beat the actuarial tables. Whoever inherits this hood after me will appreciate the repeat business."

Before Jax could answer, a group of drunken ore-haulers stumbled between their table and the aisle; when they cleared a second later, the bench opposite him was empty, and the rear VIP curtain was just beginning to part.

Jax didn't finish the dregs of his glass. He snatched up his slate, pulled his collar high over his cybernetic eye, and slipped out through the side kitchen door into the noisy, steam-choked bowels of the Lower Concourse.

Then, he ran.

Through the curved, claustrophobic arteries of Port Nowhere, his boots rang against iron deck-gratings slick with condensation. Because the habitat had been hollowed out of an irregular rubble-pile asteroid over generations of lawless expansion, the corridors didn't follow sane architectural rules; they twisted along ancient iron veins, climbing inward toward the spin axis where the artificial gravity bled away from a heavy trudge to a long, bounding half-float. Flickering neon ideograms in Mandarin, Anglic, and Xylossian trade-glyphs blurred past above open-flame noodle kiosks, unlicensed cyber-clinics, and pawn cages stacked with patched EVA suits.

"Captain," Mite’s voice crackled urgently through his wrist-cuff as Jax vaulted a stairwell railing in quarter-gravity, dropping twenty feet down a radial access shaft toward the outer docking ring. "Harbor Security Team Four has just arrived at the outer bulkhead of Berth 44-Sub-C. They have connected a hydraulic power-pack to the external umbilical collar and are initializing the Stage-One magnetic impound clamp. Estimated time to physical lock-down is three minutes and forty seconds."

"Don't wait for me to get in the chair, Mite!" Jax panted, his lungs burning as he hit the spin-ward deck at a dead sprint. "Start hot-prepping the primary fusion bottle right now! Bypass the pre-ignition checks!"

"Bypassing pre-ignition checks on an eighty-year-old magnetic-pinch coil carries a fourteen percent risk of plasma back-flash," Mite noted primly. "However, given that the alternative is becoming the property of Mr. Volkov’s scrap-metal division, I am bridging the safeties now."

Jax rounded the final dog-leg of Sub-Corridor C and skidded to a halt behind a stacked pallet of empty deuterium flasks.

Thirty meters ahead, at the end of the narrow jetty, the airlock of Berth 44-Sub-C was bathed in the amber glare of a Port Authority seizure beacon. Two dock-enforcers in yellow exo-frames were crouched beside the boarding collar, wrestling a massive, C-shaped titanium locking boot over the Void Mite’s main umbilical flange, while a third guard stood by the wall terminal with an arc-welder, waiting to spot-weld the manual release shut.

Through the thick quartz window of the jetty, Jax could see the nose of his ship clinging to the asteroid's outer skin.

Even in his most sentimental moments, he couldn't have called the Void Mite pretty. She had started life almost nine decades ago as a heavy-duty ice-tug before passing through the hands of four bankrupt prospectors, two blockade runners, and finally Jax and Ria. Her blunt, slab-sided hull was dominated by the oversized Mandate Navy sensor prow they had grafted onto her bow—making her look like a rusted bulldog with a bayonet strapped to its snout. Her starboard thruster pod was three shades darker than the port, patched with mismatched hexagonal tiles of ablative ceramic scavenged from a half-dozen bone-yards. She was ugly, cranky, and held together by love and spite.

She was also the last piece of Ria still breathing in the universe, and nobody was putting a chain on her while Jax had two hands.

"Mite," Jax whispered, drawing the plasma torch from his thigh holster and thumbing the regulator past the safety stop. "Can you vent the jetty’s fire-suppression nitrogen line into this corridor?"

"Technically, tampering with municipal safety infrastructure carries a five-hundred-credit fine," Mite replied. "Venting line four... now."

Above the heads of the three dock-enforcers, a high-pressure cast-iron valve in the ceiling shrieked and blew its gasket.

Instantly, a blizzard of freezing liquid-nitrogen vapor erupted downward into the narrow jetty, dropping visibility to zero and flash-freezing the condensation on the floor into a sheet of ice.

The guard with the arc-welder yelled in surprise, stumbling backward on the slick grating—and Jax was already airborne.

Launching himself off the cargo pallet in the low peripheral gravity, Jax sailed twenty meters through the white fog, his left optic switching seamlessly to infrared. The three men glowed like orange silhouettes in the mist. Jax hit the deck plates boots-first between the two exo-riggers, didn't waste a second fighting their armor, and jammed the white-hot jet of his overcharged plasma torch straight through the main hydraulic feed-hose of the impound clamp.

Boiling red hydraulic fluid sprayed into the nitrogen mist, flashing into a greasy pink cloud of ice crystals as the clamp's jaws seized half-closed.

"Hey—!" the lead rigger roared, swinging a pneumatic wrench the size of a fire hydrant at Jax’s head.

Jax ducked under the whistling iron arm, planted his boot against the rigger’s knee to send the top-heavy exo-frame crashing onto the ice, and slapped his palm against the Void Mite’s outer airlock override pad.

The hatch snapped open. Jax dove headfirst into the microgravity of the boarding tube, hit the inner emergency-cycle switch with his elbow, and listened to the outer pressure door slam shut a fraction of a second before a pneumatic rivet-gun hammered three dents into the exterior titanium.

He didn't pause to catch his breath. Hauling himself hand-over-hand down the three-meter access spine into the cramped, oil-scented cylinder of the flight deck, he kicked into the worn leather pilot’s couch, yanked the five-point crash webbing tight across his bruised ribs, and jammed his data-slate into the console.

"They’ve got the outer collar half-booted, Mite!" Jax barked, his fingers flying across the overhead panel as the ship’s ancient fusion reactor roared to a shaky idle behind the aft bulkhead. "If we wait for the docking ring to cycle normally, they’ll weld us to the rock!"

"I anticipated your distaste for paperwork, Captain," Mite’s voice chimed from the cockpit speakers, accompanied by the tiny, pea-sized sphere of cyan light materializing above the throttle quadrant. "I have routed the auxiliary battery bus directly into the explosive shear-bolts on the docking flange. However, before we commit an act of felony harbor-destruction from which there is no legal return, I strongly advise we examine the telemetry packet you just purchased."

Outside the hull, a heavy, metallic THUD-CLANG reverberated through the forward frame as the dock-crew hooked a secondary winch-cable onto the nose ring.

"Read it while you arm the bolts!" Jax snapped, flipping the red safety covers off the manual thruster yokes.

Cooling fans screamed behind the main instrument board as Mite’s optical processors chewed through Glitch’s polymorphic wrappers. Above the console, the holo-emitter stuttered, coughed a spark of blue static, and projected a three-dimensional chart into the cabin.

It was a high-density gravimetric rendering of the outer shoreline of LDN 1622—a vast, swirling curtain of molecular hydrogen and carbon soot swallowing the stars along the entire starboard horizon of the system. Seventeen astronomical units past the lightless dust wall, inside a lethal pocket of colliding gas filaments tagged OMC-1 North, a crimson dot pulsed like a heartbeat.

Beneath the reticle scrolled the fragmented black-box log of the HD-Perseus:

[...SOURCE CONFIRMED ARTIFICIAL... ANOMALOUS NEUTRINO EMISSION... PATTERNED FIVE-PULSE CADENCE, ZERO DECAY... MASS SIGNATURE CONSISTENT WITH CLASS-3 PRECURSOR STRUCTURE... EXTREME GRAVIMETRIC SHEAR IN SECTOR... HULL INTEGRITY FAILING... RECOMMEND IMMEDIATE WITHDRAWAL...]

Beside the text, a waveform graph unfolded—and Jax’s breath caught in his throat.

He leaned forward against his straps, the crimson light of the hologram reflecting off the rim of his left optic. His internal lenses zoomed in on the Mandate-compatible interferometer trace: five sharp spikes of neutrino flux, repeating every four seconds with atomic-clock precision against a wall of chaotic nebular noise, accompanied by a narrow-band gravimetric wake that looked like a subterranean river cutting through the dust.

"It’s real," Jax whispered, his mouth suddenly bone-dry. "A Class-Three Precursor megastructure. Alive and transmitting. And look at that secondary trace, Mite—the neutrino pulses are carving a laminar shear-channel right through the dust wall. With our Mandate cutter prow, we can actually read that channel and surf it in!"

"Captain, please listen to the mathematics before your greed overrides your brainstem," Mite said, and every trace of dry irony vanished from the AI’s voice, replaced by flat, clinical terror. "I am cross-referencing the seventeen-AU distance to OMC-1 North against our fourteen-percent fuel reserve and the sixty-seven-hour lifespan of our failing CO2 capacitor. To reach those coordinates on our remaining deuterium requires a continuous, four-hour insertion burn at two-point-one Gs—consuming ninety-six percent of our reaction mass right at the edge of the dust wall."

"Leaving us four percent for attitude adjustment inside the cloud," Jax said, his eyes locked on the five-beat pulse.

"Leaving us zero percent to brake, reverse course, or escape!" Mite countered sharply. "Once the main torch runs dry, we will be an unpowered projectile hurtling into a gravitational hurricane at three hundred kilometers per second! Even if our Mandate sensor prow allows us to ride the neutrino wake on cold-gas thrusters without being crushed into foil by four-G tidal shears, the ballistic drift across two and a half billion kilometers of soot will take ninety-eight days!"

"Then we ration the air and stretch the scrubbers."

"We cannot stretch a ruptured capacitor for ninety-eight days, Jax!" The cyan pea flared bright white above the console—the only time the AI ever dropped his title. "To keep that dielectric core from blowing out before we reach the target, I will have to shut down the galley, kill ninety percent of the avionics, and drop cabin temperature to minus-twelve degrees Celsius for three consecutive months! You will be starving in a freezing dark closet in the middle of an unmapped graveyard, praying that an alien ruin nobody has ever seen alive somehow possesses a pressurized docking bay and compatible fusion fuel at the end of a one-way chute! My Monte Carlo simulation places our probability of surviving arrival at less than 1.2%—and our probability of ever returning to human space at a number indistinguishable from zero!"

Silence hung in the tiny cockpit for two heartbeats, broken only by the muffled crunch of the hydraulic impound clamp outside biting deeper into the outer docking collar, and the wail of a Port Authority klaxon winding up down the jetty.

Jax looked down at the console.

He looked at the four hundred and twenty credits left on his slate. He thought of the cold light in the eyes of Volkov’s auditors. He thought of Ria’s laugh frozen behind missing scan-lines, and the years he had spent slowly bleeding to death one dirty scrap-haul at a time, waiting for the syndicate to finally take her ship away from him.

A slow, wild, utterly fatalistic grin spread across his grease-stained face—the expression Ria used to call his dead-man's tell right before he shoved his last stack of chips into the center of the pot.

"One point two percent," Jax murmured, his left hand wrapping around the cold-gas attitude stick while his right hovered over the crimson shear-bolt detonator. "Do you have any idea what a single Class-Three Precursor data-core fetches, Mite? If there’s an active installation sitting at the bottom of that well, we won't just pay off Volkov. We’ll fly back out of the dark in gold-plated boots."

"And the ninety-eight-point-eight percent probability that we freeze to death in the dark long before we get there?" Mite asked quietly.

"We’ve been dying by millimeters for years, buddy," Jax said softly. "I’m done dying on the installment plan. If we’re going out, we’re going out swinging at a god."

Outside the forward bullseye, the visor of a Port Authority rigger was suddenly pressed against the glass, his eyes widening as he saw the Void Mite’s attitude thrusters spitting white pre-ignition frost inside the pressurized jetty.

"Blow the collar, Mite," Jax growled, and slammed his palm down on the explosive release.

CRACK-THUMP.

Six shaped charges ringed around the Void Mite’s forward docking flange detonated in simultaneous sequence, shearing the titanium umbilical bolts clean in half and blasting the half-clamped hydraulic boot backward into the jetty in a hurricane of decompressing station air, shattered glass, and swirling nitrogen snow.

Before the emergency blast-shutters on the asteroid’s skin could even slam down, Jax yanked back on the cold-gas sticks.

Driven by the violent pneumatic kick of the breached airlock and four screaming jets of compressed nitrogen, the ugly, eighty-year-old bulk of the Void Mite shot backward out of the docking trench like a cork out of a bottle.

"Harbor defense batteries on Pier Four and Pier Six are tracking us!" Mite reported as the ship tumbled backward into the blinding floodlights of the outer anchorage, dodging between the rusted hulls of two moored ore-barges while red targeting lasers licked across her mismatched ceramic tiles. "Spin-gravity Coriolis shear is pulling us toward the lower gantry!"

"Don't fight the spin—ride it!" Jax snarled, kicking the starboard yaw pedals hard to the floor.

Instead of burning upward into the open radar cone of the station's point-defense turrets, Jax let the asteroid’s rotational momentum sling the Void Mite downward into the jagged canyon of the equatorial trench. For forty heart-stopping seconds, he flew the seventy-ton tug like a dirt-bike through a drainage ditch—skimming ten meters above rusted conveyor towers and silent strip-mining buckets, using four billion tons of spinning nickel-iron to mask his thermal signature from the harbor guns.

Then the jagged lip of the asteroid’s south pole fell away beneath their belly, and the diamond-dusted velvet of the Orion Spur opened up ahead.

Barely a few light-hours out across the outer shoals, swallowing half the heavens like an ink-black cliff rising out of the dark, hung the lightless wall of the Shipbreaker’s Graveyard.

"We are almost clear of the station's horizon shadow, Captain," Mite announced, the cyan sphere dimming to emergency power-save mode as the cabin heaters clicked off and the first breath of deep-space cold began to seep through the deck plates. "Insertion vector for LDN 1622 is locked. Four hours of main-drive acceleration fuel remaining. Once you advance that lever, there is no turning back."

Jax didn't look in the rear camera at the shrinking lights of Port Nowhere. Behind him lay nothing but a rigged ledger and a cold slab. Ahead waited three months of freezing darkness, a five-beat heartbeat in the abyss, and a one-in-a-hundred shot at everything.

Jax wrapped his hand around the main fusion throttle and punched it.
